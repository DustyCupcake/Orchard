# 🌳 Orchard

*A new way to get things done together, built for Peach Please — and for anyone else who recognizes the problem.*

---

## The problem

Every year, at Peach Please as much as anywhere, the same thing happens: a small handful of people end up carrying far more than their share. Not because nobody else is willing but because it isn't clear what needs doing. Or the person coordinating ends up spending more time chasing status updates than the work itself would take, and nothing becomes visible until it's already a crisis. A task quietly stalls for weeks and nobody notices until it's too late to fix calmly.

There's a quieter, more specific version of this too. Someone takes on a role because they're genuinely excited about a piece of what it involves and the role comes bundled with everything else that's traditionally attached to it as well. Ideally they'd hand off the parts that aren't their thing. In practice, a fixed role doesn't have a clean way to delegate just a slice of itself — there's no unit smaller than "the whole role" to actually hand someone. So the parts nobody's excited about quietly don't get done, or they land on whoever's already paying enough attention to notice.

And a lot of what actually makes camp run lives only in a few people's heads. When those people step back or get busy that knowledge goes with them.

None of that is because Peach Please's leads haven't cared enough or worked hard enough. It's what happens to *any* group doing real work together without a way to make that work visible and shareable. Orchard is an attempt to actually fix that, not just ask people to try harder.

---

## The core idea: work, not roles

Most ways of organizing a group start from *positions* — you're the kitchen lead, you're on the build team. Orchard starts from *tasks* instead. A task is a real, sized, ownable piece of work with a clear picture of what "done" looks like. Roles only exist as a description of what someone currently owns — a snapshot, not a fixed set that follow a person around and are passed on to their successor.

That also dissolves the bundling problem from above: the parts of a role nobody's excited about are just separate tasks now, sitting on the board where someone else can actually see them and pick them up — not silently glued to a role someone else is already busy with. And anyone looking for a way to help and get involved can see what needs doing.

That flips the default. Instead of being assigned things by whoever's in charge, you **claim** work that fits you. Instead of a fixed hierarchy, coordination becomes about making sure everything has a willing owner — not about telling people how to do it.

## Finding and claiming work

**Your view is a few things that fit, not a wall of everything undone.** You tell the system a bit about your strengths and how you like to work, and it surfaces tasks that match — not the entire unclaimed pile. Tasks that need something rarer — a specific skill, a language, someone who's done this before — get pushed a little harder toward the people who could actually take them.

**High-stakes or specialized tasks get a short browse window instead of a first-click grab.** If you're the only one interested when the window closes, it's yours — no extra step. If two people want the same single-person task, you're both told plainly: you're both interested, go have a conversation. You can see each other's contact info for exactly that reason. From there you can split the difference a few ways — one of you steps back, you open a second slot together, or one of you joins as a learner instead of a co-equal claim (more on that below). Nobody's forced to fight over a button, and the platform never picks a winner for you.

**Bigger tasks can take more than one person, and asking for help is a real, visible act.** If a task turns out bigger than expected, the person holding it can open another slot rather than pretending they've got it handled. Or they can break off a specific piece as its own card — an honest, concrete "I need help with this part," which is a lot more useful than silently struggling or handing the whole thing back.

**Some tasks are worth learning before you're the one holding them.** Anyone can join a task specifically to shadow it — no pressure to already be able to do the thing, just to watch and pick it up alongside whoever's currently doing it. It's a good place to start if you're new or unsure, and when the person who's been holding a task is ready to step back, whoever's been shadowing it is a natural, obvious next owner — though never an automatic one; the task still opens up for anyone to claim.

**Coordinators match, they don't command.** Their job is making sure every task has someone real behind it — actively suggesting a good fit, checking in if something looks like it's getting heavy. And if you want to talk to your coordinator about a task, there's one button for that — you don't have to know in advance whether you want help, a co-worker, or just to think out loud. They reach out and the conversation goes from there. And on the rare occasion a task genuinely needs doing but nobody who's technically qualified is willing right now, a coordinator can step in and let someone else take it anyway — visibly, with a reason attached, not a quiet workaround.

**"Who's got room" is a real question the platform can answer, not just a guess.** Counting tasks alone is misleading — three quick errands and one sprawling multi-week build can both look like "three tasks" if all that's counted is the number. So every task also carries a rough sense of its own size, and that's what coordinators actually see added up: not a task tally, but something closer to real load. That's what turns "who's got room to take on one more thing" from a guess into something a coordinator can see and act on, and it's part of what feeds a good fitted suggestion.

**A task can carry its own dates, too — as many as actually matter, each one named by whoever adds it.** A deposit due, a delivery expected, a headcount that has to go to the caterer by a certain day — not one fixed "due date" field, but whatever markers the work actually has. Like everything else with a date in Orchard, a milestone can be pinned to an actual day or set relative to the season or a phase, so it stays meaningful even if the schedule around it shifts. Whoever's currently holding the task can add or edit these freely; if someone else wants to add one, it waits for a nod from the current holder first — unless nobody's claimed the task yet, in which case anyone can add one outright.

**If a task is truly nobody's favorite, that gets an honest conversation instead of just lingering.** Rotate it as a shared duty, find someone willing to trade for it, or the people running things consciously decide to absorb it or let it go. What doesn't happen is silence.

**Nothing silently falls through.** A task that's been sitting too long flags quietly at first, then more visibly, so the group notices before it becomes an emergency — not after. But coordinators are explicitly not the fall-back person to do a task if someone does not step up. If a coordinator goes to self-assign a flagged or unclaimed task, they get a gentle check first — *are you sure there isn't someone better suited?* — a nudge, not a block, but enough to stop a coordinator quietly defaulting to taking everything on themselves, whether that's because it's easier than placing someone else or because they haven't noticed how much they're already carrying. There's also a quiet, no-names-attached way to flag that something feels off about a task — looks stalled, might need a hand — for the smaller moments that don't call for a full conversation.

## Joining — from application to your first task

The platform also eases the process of joining, gives visibility to every step and lets more of the community get involved in the process. Most people apply the ordinary way: you apply, and people in the community with the recruitment task read it and want to talk with you. Scheduling that call doesn't mean picking from one narrow slot — everyone gives their real availability, and the system finds where enough of them overlap. Two people from camp sit down with you, not one person rubber-stamping a form.

There's also a lighter path in, for the case where someone already in camp knows you. An existing member can send you a private, one-time invite link — tagged with your name so they can keep track if they're inviting a few people at once — and let the system know they think you're a good fit and personally know you. That's a real, private vouching signal, worth more than just checking a box on a public form, and depending on how much it's worth to the community, it can lighten or skip parts of the usual evaluation.

If it's a yes, you get paired with an experienced member to accompany you from day one — not a formality, a real relationship that carries through onboarding and the event itself. They're the one who notices if something about how you're settling in is worth a check-in, and follows up as a person, not as an automated flag. If you came in through someone's invite, that person is the natural first suggestion for who accompanies you — you already have some kind of relationship to build on.

Your first time opening the platform isn't a blank board. You tell it a little about your strengths and how you like to work, and a small handful of tasks that fit you show up right away. The goal, plainly: your first session should end with you having claimed something, not just toured a system. From there, finishing one task nudges you toward the next — a small, real way to grow into more over time if you want to, at your own pace.

Some information about you doesn't belong to any one form or moment — an emergency contact, your pronouns, how you're planning to get to the event. Rather than asking on the application, again at onboarding, and a third time in a last-minute logistics form, Orchard asks it once and remembers. It lives on your own profile where you can update it any time things change, and if you don't know yet — how you're getting there, say, months out — you can say so and it'll come back around closer to the date instead of forcing a guess now. How much you can take on works the same way, with one twist: since that answer changes across a season — Procurement and Build week rarely ask the same of you — the question comes back around for whichever phase is coming up next, rather than being asked once and forgotten. If there's a real gap between what you said you had room for and what you've taken on, your coordinator can see that — as a simple flag by default, or as real numbers if you've opted to share that level of detail — feeding a good fitted suggestion your way, or telling a coordinator to ease off before you get overloaded. And if you haven't answered at all for the phase that's coming up, that's visible to your coordinator too, whatever your privacy setting — not to embarrass anyone, just so a quick "hey, how are you set for the next few weeks?" can happen while there's still time to act on it.

If a Community chooses to — this is a real setting, not assumed — joining a branch is part of this too: Fruit, Wood, whatever the branches are called. That comes with an expectation, not just an invitation: branch calls are a thing you're expected to show up to. If you can't make a particular one, someone follows up — not to guilt anyone, just to make sure nobody quietly drifts out of contact without a person noticing. Calls like these can also carry a shared agenda anyone can add to beforehand and a written summary afterward for anyone who couldn't make it — useful for keeping a working call on track, not just a courtesy for absentees.

## Running a season

Someone has to start the engine, and Orchard makes that honest instead of invisible. One person opens up the handful of truly critical roles for the coming season — and by doing that, they've implicitly said "I'll hold this if nobody else does." That's the actual truth of how a season starts, made visible rather than assumed.

From there, coordination roles fill in, a kickoff call catches anything that's still open, and then everything else opens up to the whole camp. Nothing coordination-critical gets left empty going into the season.

A season can also have a real cap on how many people it holds, if that matters for your event — a venue's limit, say. Returning members get a window to claim their spot before the doors open more broadly to new applicants, so someone who's been coming for years doesn't lose out to timing alone, but that window doesn't hold room open forever — once it closes, everyone's competing for whatever's left on equal footing.

And it's not just for the one big annual production. A reunion weekend, a fundraiser, going to a different burn — all of these can get the same lightweight treatment: start from last year's board as a head start, or build a smaller one just for the occasion, with the same people and the same tools. A Community that wants to can even name these different kinds of gatherings — a Season versus a Reunion, say — mainly so "how many of these has this member actually done" can count the real productions without a lighter get-together quietly inflating the number.

A season doesn't need a start and end date to exist, but giving it one unlocks something useful: everything else with a date — a phase, a task milestone, a freestanding event — can then be described relative to it instead of pinned to a specific day. Say a phase is supposed to start two weeks before doors open; set it up that way once, and if the season's actual start date ever shifts, that phase shifts with it automatically, instead of someone having to remember to go update every downstream date by hand. It works the other direction too — drag a phase or milestone to a new day on the calendar and the underlying relationship recalculates rather than your edit getting silently lost the next time something upstream moves. None of this is required — a season with no dates at all still works exactly as it does today — but it's there for anyone whose season actually runs on a calendar.

Once you're there, the platform steps back on purpose. Everything locks into a read-only reference rather than an active thing to manage, because being present at camp shouldn't mean staring at a screen — there are still tasks and shifts happening on-site, but most people never need to open the app to do them. Instead, whoever's holding the specific task of running that bridge acts as the conduit: checking the app so it doesn't have to be everyone, writing status up on a whiteboard, flagging gaps in person. The information still flows both ways, it just moves through a person and a marker instead of a phone. Everything reconciles back once the event winds down.

## Seeing where you stand

It's natural to wonder, partway through a season, how you're doing — is this just a busy stretch for everyone, or are you carrying more than most? Your own view answers that concretely: what you've already finished, what you're actively holding right now, and what you've already signed up for down the line, set next to the average for people actually active this season. That's yours by default, and it stays that way unless you decide otherwise — there's a toggle if you'd rather the rest of camp could see it too.

Zoom out past any one person and there's a whole-camp picture as well, on the same dashboard that already shows who's coming this season and the shape of who they are. Nothing about any one person's numbers — just the aggregate: how work is spread across the group right now, at a glance. It's not there to rank anyone. It's the same instinct behind everything else here — a season should be legible to itself, not something only the person coordinating it can see the shape of.

That same dashboard shows how each branch is doing, too — at a glance, nothing more than on track, needs attention, or struggling. If a branch's tasks are quietly piling up unaddressed, that stops being invisible to everyone but its own coordinator; it's the kind of thing someone looking for where to pitch in might notice, not just something a coordinator has to catch by themselves. Anyone doing coordination work sees the fuller picture behind that — which flags, how many — the same way the rest of camp sees the simple version.

## What doesn't get lost: notes on a task, and knowledge for everyone

Tasks describe the goal, not the steps — that's deliberate, and it stays true. The person who claims a task should be free to bring their own approach, not follow someone else's checklist. But how people did something before is useful information, and hiding it away in a separate system just means it never gets used.

So there's a place for it, right on the task, clearly separate from what the task actually asks for: a running note anyone can improve over time, plus individual comments and links — the form that worked last year, a photo of the sign that got printed, where the good deal on ordering was. Optional. Visible without hunting for it. Never mistaken for "the instructions."

Not everything worth knowing belongs to one task, though — how the platform works, camp policy, the answer to a question three different people have asked over the years. That lives in a general, browsable space anyone can contribute to, on by default because losing this kind of knowledge is the exact problem Orchard exists to solve. A question with no answer yet is a perfectly normal way for a page to start — someone asks, it sits until someone who knows answers it, and it becomes a real page from there. If the honest answer is "this already exists," pointing at the existing page is just as good as writing a new one.

## Asking the community something

Take breakfast ordering: whoever's planning it needs to know what people actually want to eat. That's not a debate — it's a quick question. Rather than constant one-off pings, these questions collect through the week and go out together in a short batch — a five-minute thing you do once a week, not a stream of notifications to manage.

For bigger or more time-sensitive things — where the barrio ends up on the map, say, or a real structural question about how camp's run — there's a heavier version. Someone proposes it, there's a window where people can add to it, real notice before voting opens, then a voting period. And for anything genuinely urgent, that gets announced the way anything urgent does — the group chat everyone already checks, not waiting on a weekly batch or an app notification. What comes back informs the decision; it doesn't automatically override however the community actually makes decisions like that — it's a tool to make sure asking is real, not a substitute for the community's own judgment.

## When something's wrong

There's a safe, low-friction way to say "I need to talk to someone" — you don't have to write a full account of what happened just to start a conversation. And if your concern involves someone who happens to be on the team that handles this, you can rule them out completely — genuinely, invisibly, not just "denied access." The same goes the other way: if someone on that team realizes they're too close to a situation to be neutral — even if they're not directly involved, a close relationship can be enough — they can step themselves back, or someone else on the team can step them back, before they ever see it.

Feedback after the event can also surface things that never came up in the moment. Someone has the job of actually reading those responses and following up — a human decision every time, never an automatic escalation.

## Privacy, consent, and who's accountable

Some of what a Community asks people to share is genuinely sensitive — a health condition, an allergy, who you love, an emergency contact. None of it gets collected without asking you first, specifically, for that one purpose — sharing an allergy with whoever's planning food isn't the same yes as appearing in an event photo, and they're tracked separately. You can take any of them back whenever you want, and when you do, whoever could see it actually loses access, not just a note saying you'd rather they hadn't.

Emergency access is the one deliberate exception, and it works differently on purpose. If something's genuinely wrong, any member can surface someone's emergency-only contact info without asking permission first — a safety net that could quietly stop working because of an unchecked box wouldn't be much of one. Both people find out it happened either way, and there's always a reason attached, even if it's added after the fact rather than before.

None of this floats free of someone accountable, either. A Community running this platform stands behind its handling of people's data as a real, responsible body — not an informal understanding that happens to rest on whoever's running the server this year.

## Where this actually lives: phone and laptop

Most of what's above is meant to work from your phone, because that's where most people will actually open this — checking your dashboard, claiming or releasing a task, answering the week's batch of questions. The heavier tools — laying out camp on a real floorplan, working the budget, the fuller coordination view a branch coordinator needs — will work on the phone but they're built for a larger screen, the same way you'd already reach for a bigger screen to do that kind of work regardless of what app it's in.

## Settings — yours and the Community's

Two very different things both end up called "settings," and it's worth keeping them apart. Yours — notifications, how you like to be contacted, your availability — is always just yours. The Community's — branches, tiers, which optional pieces are switched on, the deeper structural choices — is different, and who gets to touch it shouldn't just be whoever happened to install the thing, or a fixed group that never turns over.

So that access is itself a task, the same as everything else here — call it **Admin**. Early in a season's kickoff, alongside the other roles that need someone behind them before things get moving, anyone can put themselves forward for it — but taking it on isn't a simple first-click claim. Enough of the community actually has to back you first. However many people clear that bar is how many Admins there are that season — no fixed number to hit or defend, so a small, quiet gathering naturally ends up with a couple of Admins instead of a dozen, sized to who's really there and trusted, not to a seat count decided once and forgotten. Whether a given gathering re-opens the role at all is nothing special either — it's simply whether Admin was one of the things that gathering's task set included in the first place, the same as any other role. A full season including it means a fresh vote of confidence, nobody just coasting on last time's trust; a quiet reunion weekend that never included it in the first place just leaves whoever already holds it holding it, undisturbed.

That's the right level of trust for the ordinary, day-to-day settings — the ones already expected to shift casually as a Community grows and changes. The bigger, harder-to-reverse choices — the kind that are really a migration dressed up as a toggle — deserve something wider than whoever happens to be active in one particular season, especially a small one. For those, the expectation is that the *wider* community weighs in, not just this cycle's slice of it, before anyone acts on it — using the same open-to-everyone discussion-and-vote tool described above. Nothing forces that to happen; it's a strong norm, the same trust the rest of this document runs on, not a lock the software snaps shut. But it means the biggest decisions about how a Community works are never something a handful of people who happened to be around one quiet weekend get to make alone.

## Everything else, briefly

Orchard also handles the things you'd expect a camp platform to handle, just built on the same underlying ideas:

- **Calendar** — everything with a date in one place: phase and milestone dates for the season you're in, branch calls, workshops, input-round and assembly deadlines, birthdays for anyone who's chosen to share theirs — each its own layer you can show or hide. Events you create are just yours until you choose to share them; share with a branch or the whole camp and it goes out as an invitation each person accepts or declines for themselves, not something anyone else decides on your behalf.
- **Budget** — proposals, ranked voting on priorities, real visibility into where money's going.
- **Events & workshops** — proposing, scheduling, avoiding conflicts, publishing.
- **Spatial planning** — laying out camp, sleeping arrangements, drag-and-drop with real dimensions, not just a text list. Move your own tent and it's provisional until whoever's running the layout signs off — never blocked, but never silently final either.
- **Shifts** — the recurring stuff that's never really "done," kept separate from one-off tasks.
- **Finding a time to meet** — the same tool that schedules an intro call also handles branch calls and any other "when can enough of us actually make it" problem. You paint the times you're free on a simple grid rather than typing them in, with everyone's real availability collected blind so nobody's just picking around what's already shown.

One thing runs through all of these rather than sitting inside any one of them: access follows the task, not the role. Claim the task that needs it, and whatever it takes to actually do the job — someone's dietary info to plan the menu, say — opens up along with it. Step away from the task, and so does that access. Nobody needs to be handed a permanent title just to get what one piece of work actually requires.

## What's actually different here

Most tools built for running a camp or similar group start from administration: applications, schedules, budgets — logistics for people who already know what they're doing. Orchard starts from a different question: how does a piece of work actually find a willing person, and how does a group stay legible to itself without depending on one person holding it all in their head.

Everything above follows from that one starting point. It won't look like every other camp tool, because it isn't trying to solve the same problem.
